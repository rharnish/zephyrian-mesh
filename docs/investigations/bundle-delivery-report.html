The last hop

Telemetry delivery sat stubbornly near 40–50% in a mesh where almost every balloon held a genuine route to the ground. Two plausible explanations turned out to be wrong. The real limit is about two dozen balloons — and widening what they do in a single tower contact recovers 16 points.

01 — the question

A mesh that knows the way, and still can’t deliver

At the default configuration, 96–99% of balloons hold a live route belief and the mesh sits comfortably above the percolation threshold. Yet only about half of all telemetry bundles reach a tower. The obvious suspects — congestion, stale routes, bad paths — had each been proposed and, in two earlier passes, wrongly confirmed.

So this round started with instrumentation rather than a hypothesis: every wake slot where a balloon held a bundle was classified by why it did or didn’t move, and every bundle’s hop count was recorded at delivery and at expiry.

02 — first correction

The metric was biased, but only slightly

delivered / originated counts every bundle still legitimately in flight at the cutoff as a failure. Measuring against bundles that actually resolved removes that bias — and the correction is small: 1.4 points over 4000 rounds, 3.0 points over 2000. Worth fixing, but it is not the story.

03 — where the losses go

Essentially every lost bundle expires

Loop drops and hop-budget drops are rounding errors — 166 and 4 respectively against ~11,000 deliveries. Bundles are not being destroyed by the protocol. They are running out of time while someone holds them. That reframes the question: not why are bundles lost, but why are they slow.

A bundle’s entire life is bundle_max_age_rounds / beacon_interval_rounds = 30 wake slots. Above the percolation threshold the stall rate is only 24–27%, leaving ~22 usable hops against a true median depth of 6.5. On the slot budget alone, these bundles should arrive comfortably. They don’t — so the budget is not what binds.

Note also what is absent: stale next hop never exceeds 0.3% in the shipped protocol. The hold-don’t-drop path — the mechanism that makes this delay-tolerant rather than merely lossy — is almost never exercised at these densities. Links outlive beliefs by a wide margin.

04 — hypothesis killed

Beliefs are not inflated

The leading structural suspect was should_adopt: a fresher beacon wave wins regardless of hop count, so a balloon three hops from Nairobi can adopt a fifteen-hop belief from Svalbard purely because that wave is newer. With twelve towers beaconing independently, that should inflate effective path length badly.

It doesn’t. Believed depth tracks the omniscient shortest-path depth from bin/mesh_depth within about a hop — and at coefficient 3.57 the balloons believe they are closer than the true median, not further.

The ablation confirms it from the other side. Routing on hop count first, with freshness only as a tie-break, helps near the percolation threshold — where it raises the grounded fraction from 85.5% to 92.2% — but hurts above it, dropping completion from 56.0% to 50.5% at the default density. Freshness-first is not the bug.

05 — the answer

About two dozen balloons

A tower at 30 m has a 23 km radio horizon. A balloon at 13 km has roughly 470 km. So the tower–balloon link reaches about 490 km — against 940 km for balloon-to-balloon. The ground stations are, in radio terms, nearly invisible.

Measured directly rather than inferred from that geometry: at any moment an average of 23 balloons out of 1200 can hear a tower. Each can pass exactly one bundle per duty-cycle slot. That is the entire delivery capacity of the planet: 

~23 tower-adjacent balloons / 5 rounds per duty cycle ≈ 4.7 deliveries per round

Observed throughput plateaus a little below that, at ~3.1/round — the gap being the slots where a tower-adjacent balloon is awake but happens to be holding nothing. The decisive observation is not the exact figure but its flatness: raising origination 7× leaves delivery at 3.1/round and simply grows the denominator, while lowering it below the ceiling pushes completion to 93% with no protocol change whatsoever.

Nothing about queue depth, belief lifetime, or route selection can move a flat line — which is exactly why three rounds of tuning those parameters produced nothing.

The same relationship holds across mesh density, with one instructive exception. Delivery rises with density mostly because a longer radio horizon puts more balloons in tower range. But below the percolation threshold the mesh delivers only 27% of its last-hop ceiling — there the bottleneck really is upstream, in balloons that hold no route at all. The crossover is the percolation threshold itself: below it the mesh is the constraint, above it the ground link is.

06 — what it means

This is a finding, not a defect

Nothing here is behaving incorrectly. A constellation with twelve ground stations, radios that wake once per ten simulated minutes, and antennas 30 m above the ground genuinely cannot land more than a few bundles per round. The simulation is reporting a real property of the architecture it was given.

That reframes the levers. The ones that matter all act on the last hop:

  • More towers. Delivery capacity is linear in tower-adjacent population. This is the blunt fix.
  • Taller antennas. Horizon goes as √height, and the tower’s 30 m is the small term in a 490 km link. Cheap to test.
  • Multi-bundle contact windows. The one-transmission-per-slot rule is right for a beacon and wrong for a tower contact: a balloon in tower range should drain its queue, not dribble one bundle per ten minutes. Highest leverage, costs nothing physically — implemented and measured below.
  • Satellite fallback — already scheduled as C2 slice 2. It now has a measured justification: it is not a nicety for stranded bundles, it is the release valve for a structurally saturated ground link.
Worth recording

The bottleneck was invisible for three rounds of investigation because every metric being watched described the mesh — degree, grounded fraction, stall rate, queue occupancy — and the mesh was healthy. The scarce resource was never in the frame.

07 — acting on it

The contact window, tested

The cheapest of those levers was implemented and measured: a balloon in tower range now drains up to batch.tower_contact in one contact instead of passing a single bundle per duty cycle. Setting that constant to 1 reproduces the old rule exactly, so the comparison is clean — nothing else changed.

Completion goes 51.2% → 67.4%, a gain of 16 points against a run-to-run noise band of about 8, with a monotone trend across three steps. The rule applies only to tower contacts; balloon-to-balloon handoffs still move one bundle per slot, because there the sender’s duty cycle is genuinely what rations the transmission.

And then it stops

Past a window of 4 the gain vanishes — 8 measures no better than 4. That is the expected shape, and it is the useful part of the result: once the window reaches 4 the last-hop ceiling rises to 16.6/round against 5.9/round offered, so the last hop is no longer the binding constraint. The limit moves back into the mesh, to bundles expiring before they ever reach a balloon that can see a tower.

So the value ships at 4, not 8: beyond it there is nothing to buy, and a shorter burst is a weaker claim on airtime. The next constraint is now a different problem from the one this report was about — and satellite fallback, already scheduled as C2 slice 2, is aimed squarely at it.
